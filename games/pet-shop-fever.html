Pet Shop Fever

Mobile · Time management

Pet Shop Fever is a free-to-play time-management game. Players run busy pet shops, bathing, grooming and feeding animals against the clock, then move on to new themed pet shops with new guests, rare breeds and more than 800 levels. Regular limited-time events, card collections and minigames keep it growing.

I joined Pet Shop Fever during its soft launch, as one of two game designers in an 11-person team. As the game grew into a live service, the work shifted from building the core to keeping it growing: new content, events and features, without breaking what already works.

The team's goals changed over time, and usually meant increasing retention, reducing churn, growing monetization and shipping content updates.

My contributions

  • Feature designI designed the features that carried players between sessions: collections, daily challenges and minigames.
  • LiveOps designI designed the live events, both their structure and their content.
  • Economy & balancingI mapped currency sources and sinks for each new pet shop and kept the economy balanced.
  • Level designI tuned level pacing and difficulty curves so the challenge stayed meaningful.
  • Monetization designI shaped the shop and contextual offers, tested through A/B experiments.
  • Maintenance spreadsheetsI created spreadsheets that controlled level data, the economy, events and offers. They made it easier to set up new pet shops, adjust values without depending on programming, check the balance and update events, and were used by the design team.
  • Playtesting & A/B testingI validated changes with playtesting, working in Unity and shipping with Scrum.

Challenges & solutions

Balancing goals that pull in different directions

In a live free-to-play game, the team's goals changed over time: retaining players, reducing churn, growing monetization and updating content. The challenge was meeting all of them without losing the player experience. The focus was on:

  • Experience firstDesigning difficulty, player support and offers to sustain the game, not to block it.
  • One goal, one hypothesisTurning each team goal into a design hypothesis to test, rather than a decision made on intuition.

Deciding with evidence in a live game

A live game keeps changing, and every change can help or hurt. To decide with confidence, the testing process followed these steps:

  • ObserveTracking the game's metrics, a game design responsibility, in performance spreadsheets, and analyzing player data alongside playtests.
  • Hypothesis and targeted changeProposing hypotheses and changing as few variables as possible at a time, to understand what causes each effect.
  • MeasureComparing the periods before and after each change, or testing versions against each other in A/B.
  • Learn and iterateTurning each result into the next decision. For example, a single change to the character's speed cut early-funnel churn by around 30%.

In a live game, design is never a one-time decision: it is a loop of observing, testing and learning.

Keeping the player experience strong in a long progression

With more than 800 levels, the challenge was the whole progression rather than any single level: players should feel tension, relief, fairness and progress along the entire path. The focus was on:

  • Difficulty as a journeyDesigning the difficulty curve across the progression, not level by level, and testing it.
  • Variety along the wayGiving the long journey new goals and variety, through side loops and LiveOps.
  • A store that doesn't get in the wayShaping and testing the store and the offers to support the journey instead of blocking it.

Results

During the period of work on the project, Pet Shop Fever was one of the company's top 3 most profitable games, in a portfolio of more than 100 titles.

  • Steady growthThe game's revenue kept growing steadily throughout the period, driven by the store remodel, difficulty testing and the addition of side loops and LiveOps.
  • Delivery paceAt the start of the period, releasing a new pet shop with 100 levels took around 5 months. As the team matured, that dropped to around 1 month, about five times faster, and it became possible to deliver a new pet shop and a minigame in a sprint and a half.
  • Design autonomyConfiguration spreadsheets and a testing routine let the design team tune values, check the balance and update events without depending on programming.
  • A data-guided operationPerformance-tracking spreadsheets and a routine of testing hypotheses guided the game's decisions.

A live game taught me that good design is consistency: test, learn and deliver, cycle after cycle.
